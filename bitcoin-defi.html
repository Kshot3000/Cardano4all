Bitcoin on Cardano — first ask who holds the BTC.

Bitcoin does not live on Cardano. When a Cardano app lets you lend, borrow against or trade “BTC”, some design is standing between you and the real coins on the Bitcoin chain — and the designs differ in the one way that matters most: who can stop you getting your BTC back. This guide separates the four trust models honestly: custodial wrapped BTC, watcher bridges, ZK bridgeless designs, and MuSig2 / BitVMX designs. A custodial or watcher-held asset is not trustless or native just because an app calls it Bitcoin, and no protocol is endorsed on this page — it is a map of what to check, not a recommendation. This page operates no bridge, holds no BTC, and there is nothing to deposit here.

The four models

Every way of using BTC in Cardano DeFi today fits one of these four shapes. The token in your Cardano wallet may look the same in each case; what you actually hold — and who you are trusting — is not the same at all.

  • 1. Custodial wrapped BTC

    Who holds the BTC: a company or custodian. You send real BTC to the custodian, and the custodian issues a token on Cardano that stands for it; sending the token back to the custodian is how you redeem the BTC. Wrapped-BTC services in this shape (the anetaBTC-style model) brought useful liquidity to Cardano early.

    What you are trusting: the custodian — that it holds the BTC it claims to hold, stays solvent and honest, and honours redemptions. If the custodian fails or freezes withdrawals, the token can lose its backing no matter how healthy any loan against it looks. This model is not trustless and the token is not native BTC — it is a claim on a custodian.

  • 2. Watcher bridges — Rosen Bridge

    Who holds the BTC: the bridge's guard set, under the watch of its watchers. Watchers observe the Bitcoin chain and report deposits; a separate guard set acts on those reports to release the bridged asset on Cardano, and the same machinery in reverse releases BTC on the way back. Rosen Bridge, born in the Ergo ecosystem, has bridged Bitcoin assets including Runes into Cardano this way.

    What you are trusting: the watcher and guard sets — that enough of them stay honest and online for deposits to be reported truthfully and withdrawals to be processed. That is a smaller, more distributed trust than a single custodian, but it is still trust in identified operators: a watcher bridge is not trustless, and its asset is not native BTC either.

  • 3. ZK bridgeless — BitcoinOS Grail / xBTC and Charms

    Who holds the BTC: no custodian and no operator set, by design. In the Grail design from BitcoinOS, BTC is locked on Bitcoin under conditions a zero-knowledge proof (BitSNARK, open-sourced in March 2025) can verify, and the Cardano-side token — xBTC — can be returned and the BTC withdrawn without asking a validator set for permission; the first bridgeless BTC round trip to Cardano mainnet and back has already been executed this way. Charms takes a related route, “beaming” Bitcoin-native assets such as eBTC to Cardano as native tokens.

    What you are trusting: the proof system and the code that implements it — mathematics and audits instead of operators. That is a genuinely different trust model, and “bridgeless” describes the design honestly; it does not make any given deployment risk-free. The code is young, so the questions shift from “who is the operator?” to “has this exact implementation been audited, and is the version I am using the audited one?”

  • 4. MuSig2 / BitVMX — Cardinal Protocol

    Who holds the BTC: a multisignature arrangement on Bitcoin itself. Input Output's Cardinal Protocol (announced June 2025) wraps BTC UTXOs using MuSig2 multisignatures, with BitVMX providing the verification path that lets Cardano-side lending and borrowing react if a signer misbehaves — the aim is BTC in Cardano DeFi without handing the coins to a custodian.

    What you are trusting: the signer set and the verification game — that the required signers stay available, and that the challenge-and-proof machinery would in practice be run if it were ever needed. It sits between the watcher model and the ZK model: no single custodian, but not operator-free either. In public, Cardinal is a published protocol specification; treat any product claiming to be Cardinal as its own implementation to check, not as the spec itself.

Side by side

  • Custodial wrapped BTC — BTC held by: one custodian. Getting it back needs: the custodian to process your redemption. Main way it fails: the custodian.
  • Watcher bridge (Rosen) — BTC held by: the bridge's guard set. Getting it back needs: watchers to report and guards to act. Main way it fails: the operator sets collude, fail, or stall.
  • ZK bridgeless (Grail / xBTC, Charms) — BTC held by: no operator — locked under proof-verifiable conditions. Getting it back needs: a valid proof, by design without validator consent. Main way it fails: a bug in young proof or contract code.
  • MuSig2 / BitVMX (Cardinal) — BTC held by: a Bitcoin multisig. Getting it back needs: the signer set, policed by BitVMX verification. Main way it fails: signers unavailable, or the verification path never exercised in practice.

None of the four puts actual BTC inside a Cardano wallet — Cardano tokens are claims, locks or representations whose value rests on the row above. When two apps both say “BTC”, check whether they are even using the same row before comparing their rates.

Questions to ask before trusting any of them

  • Which model is this, in the protocol's own words? If the documentation will not say plainly who holds the BTC, that is your answer.
  • How do I get BTC back to the Bitcoin chain, step by step? Whose action does each step need — a company's, a guard set's, a signer set's, or only a proof's?
  • What happens if that party stops? A custodian freezing withdrawals, watchers going offline and signers going missing are different failures with different recoveries — look for the written answer, not the reassuring one.
  • Has this exact code been audited, and is what I am using the audited version? This matters most for the youngest designs, the ZK and BitVMX ones.
  • Is the asset I receive the asset I think it is? Check the token's policy ID in your wallet against the protocol's published one — lookalike tokens are the oldest trick in DeFi.
  • Do the loan numbers still make sense? Only after the trust question: model the collateral, the LTV and the liquidation price with the hub's Bitcoin DeFi collateral modeler — with a price you assume yourself, because a healthy LTV on an asset you cannot redeem is not safety.
